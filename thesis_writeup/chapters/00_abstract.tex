Cardiac digital twins produce realistic synthetic ECGs with their generating physiology known exactly, and foundation models produce reusable ECG representations; together they promise readouts of mechanistic quantities, such as infarct territory, that clinical labels cannot supervise. This thesis tests that promise on one twin (MedalCare-XL), one real cohort (PTB-XL) and one foundation model (ECGFounder), and builds the instrument its failure turned out to require. Within the twin's domain the promise holds: infarct territory is linearly readable from the adapted model's latent space, exceeding a 54-feature clinical control by $+0.15$ macro-F$_1$ on identical recordings. Across the synthetic-to-real gap it holds only in part. Under a decision rule fixed in advance, a readout fitted purely on the twin sorts real infarcts into anterior versus inferior above its pre-registered bar, but misses the four-class bar and loses its entire advantage over the clinical control. The reason is located by a new informativeness-fidelity audit, which compares, feature by feature, how much territory information each of 54 standard ECG measurements carries in the twin against the real cohort. The twin relocates territory information across measurement blocks. Its signal sits in the ST segment; the real cohort's sits in the Q and R waves and the frontal QRS axis, with 28 confirmed blind spots and 14 spurious channels. How realistic a feature's distribution looks does not predict which. The audit's map predicts transfer out of sample, with the twin's most informative block transporting at the constant-predictor floor, and no downstream repair, channel restriction, reweighting or recalibration, recovers the loss; what transfers is a twelve-dimensional inferior-lead subspace and a zero-shot anchor frame. A floor-aware methodology for evaluating angular readouts is developed along the way. The conclusion generalises the finding: marginal realism is not informativeness fidelity, and synthetic cohorts should be audited for the information a task needs before models are trained on them.
