The audit of \S\ref{sec:audit-method} asks a question that needs no encoder: for each of the 53 distinct ECG features and the frontal QRS axis, how much four-class territory information does that measurement carry in the synthetic cohort, and how much in the real one? Figure~\ref{fig:audit} is the answer. Each point is one feature, placed by its ANOVA \etasq\ against territory in \medalcare\ (horizontal) and in \ptbxl\ (vertical), with 500-draw block-bootstrap intervals on both axes. If the simulator were informationally faithful the points would lie near the diagonal. They do not. The Q- and R-amplitude features sit far above it, the ST features far below it, and the T features close to it: the two domains agree on how much territory information the ECG carries overall, and disagree systematically about \emph{where} it is written. \tracenote{FA/f1_fidelity.json→features; fig1_points.csv; blocks readable in Fig 4.1}

The disagreement is a property of the measure-blocks, not of the individual features (Table~\ref{tab:blockinv}). Averaged within blocks, the simulator's ranking of where territory information lives is close to the reverse of reality's: ST is its most informative block (\etasq\ 0.060) and carries almost nothing in the real cohort (0.018), while Q and R are its least informative blocks (0.009 and 0.007) and carry the most in reality (0.088 and 0.069). The Spearman correlation between the two block-mean profiles is $-0.59$. At per-feature resolution the headline correlation of $-0.354$ (bootstrap interval $[-0.44, -0.25]$) looks significant under a naive test ($p=0.009$), but features within a block are not independent observations: a permutation that moves block labels as units gives $p = 0.10$, or $0.14$ when leads are also permuted within blocks, so no per-feature claim is licensed. Inside the blocks the simulator's ordering is uncorrelated with reality's (ST $-0.21$, Q $-0.28$, R $+0.04$, all $p>0.37$) or positively correlated (T $+0.71$, $p=0.010$; globals $+0.60$). I therefore state the finding at the resolution the data support: the simulator inverts the measure-block ranking of territory information, and within blocks its ordering is uncorrelated to positively correlated with reality. \tracenote{verifier tmp_v_f1-fidelity-audit_out.txt lines 18-31 (block table, -0.5873, p 0.1027/0.1408, residual +0.1313); FA/f1_fidelity.json→rank_agreement (-0.3544 [-0.4395,-0.2478], naive p 0.0086)}

Twenty-eight features, plus the axis, are confirmed blind spots: informative about territory in the real cohort while flat in the simulator, with the bootstrap interval on the difference excluding zero (Table~\ref{tab:blindspur}, upper panel). The list is dominated by the Q and R amplitudes, where 22 of the 24 lead features qualify (the Q amplitudes in V1 and V6 do not). The largest single gap is Q\_amp\_III: \etasq\ 0.006 in the simulator against 0.324 in \ptbxl, a difference of $+0.319$ with interval $[+0.290, +0.340]$.

The pattern is clinically coherent. Pathological Q waves and R-wave loss are the localisation signature of established infarction, and the limb-lead Q amplitudes that head the list (III, aVF, II) are the leads a cardiologist reads for inferior infarction. \tracenote{FA/f1_fidelity.json→lists.blind_spots_4c (29 incl. axis); features Q_amp_III diff +0.3186 [0.290,0.340]; Q/R count 22/24, Q_amp_V1 and Q_amp_V6 absent from list}

The frontal QRS axis makes the same point with two numbers and no delineator. Its circular \etasq\ against territory is 0.0020 in \medalcare\ and 0.1297 in \ptbxl\ (difference $+0.128$, interval $[+0.108, +0.149]$): a sixty-five-fold deficit on the oldest localisation channel in electrocardiography. This is not a variance artefact, because the axis is about equally dispersed in the two cohorts (circular SD $41.3^\circ$ synthetic, $37.9^\circ$ real), and the real-domain structure runs in the physiologically expected direction: inferior infarcts shift the mean axis to $17.2^\circ$ against $41.2^\circ$ for anteroseptal ones, a spread of $41.9^\circ$ across territory means, where the simulator's four territory means sit within $7.3^\circ$ of one another. The simulator reproduces the axis as a quantity and gives it almost none of the territory dependence reality gives it. \tracenote{FA/f1_fidelity.json→axis (0.00201/0.12973, diff CI, circ_sd 41.29/37.90); per-territory means MR A.6 table (17.2/41.2; spreads 7.3/41.9)}

ST is not uniformly spurious: the mid-precordial ST\_J60\_V4 and the V2--V6 average carry slightly \emph{more} information in reality ($+0.013$ and $+0.018$, both intervals above zero). Marginal realism does not predict any of this. If distributional mismatch explained the informativeness gaps, the features with the largest standardised mean differences between domains would show the largest \etasq\ gaps. The correlation between $|\mathrm{SMD}|$ and the absolute gap is $-0.28$ ($p=0.037$) taken at face value, and it is not robust: removing the twelve T-amplitude features flips it to $+0.08$ ($p=0.61$), and a block-level permutation gives $p=0.16$. The honest summary is dissociation. The T block has the worst marginals in the feature set ($|\mathrm{SMD}|$ 1.2--2.5) and is the only informativeness-matched block (\etasq\ 0.032 simulated, 0.028 real); the Q and R blocks have mostly unremarkable marginals ($|\mathrm{SMD}| < 1$) and hide the largest deficits. A validation protocol that checks distributions feature by feature would pass the features that fail this audit, and flag the ones that pass it. \tracenote{FA/f1_fidelity.json→marginal_vs_informativeness (-0.2840, p 0.0374); verifier lines 54-57 (excl-T +0.0828 p 0.607; block-perm p 0.1578); DD C6 (T |SMD| 1.2-2.5)}

Three confounds are declared where they bite. The three simulated interval durations (QRS, QT, P) are missing for 21--24\,\% of simulator rows and the missingness depends on territory ($\chi^2$ $p$ down to $3.7\times10^{-18}$), so their simulated \etasq\ is treated as unreliable and flagged in every table; in the real cohort interval missingness is unrelated to territory ($p = 0.34$--$0.94$) and the per-lead blocks are close to complete (at most 38 of 4,324 rows missing). \texttt{T\_amplitude\_mV} duplicates \texttt{T\_amp\_II} exactly in both domains and is counted once. Repeating the whole audit on the two-class collapse of the label, which removes most of the granularity mismatch between the two label constructions, reproduces the classification of every confirmed feature except one: R\_amp\_V6 is real-informative at four classes ($+0.041$) and simulator-leaning at two ($-0.017$), both intervals excluding zero, and is reported as the exception. \tracenote{FA/f1_fidelity.json→missingness (finite 4968/5089/5168 of 6547; chi2 3.75e-18; real p 0.34-0.94), addendum→duplicate_columns; features R_amp_V6 (+0.0413 [0.027,0.056] / -0.0165 [-0.022,-0.011])}

The strongest objection to the audit is that it compares \etasq\ across two label spaces that differ in kind: the simulated territory is derived from a continuous angle, the real one from diagnosis codes, and \etasq\ is sensitive to class balance. Three observations answer it. First, the map's predictions are tested out of sample in the next two sections by experiments that never compare \etasq\ across domains; readouts are fitted on simulated labels and scored on real rows, and they obey the map. A labelling artefact inside the \etasq\ comparison cannot reach into a transport experiment. Second, the two-class collapse just described shrinks the granularity mismatch and changes one feature of the confirmed set. Third, the flagship axis gap is sixty-five-fold at matched dispersion, with the real-side direction physiologically correct; no plausible class-balance perturbation spans two orders of magnitude. \tracenote{MR Part C.1 / DD Section 1 (objection and answer); sections 4.5-4.6 below}

The shape of the map has a natural clinical reading. The simulator's MI model is an acute ischaemia model: its territory signal lives in the ST segment, the acute injury current. The real cohort is dominated by established infarction (90.2\,\% of the graded records are Stadium II--III or III), whose localisation signature is the Q wave, R-wave loss and the frontal axis. The audit's blind spots and spurious channels are exactly the two sides of that contrast, and I read the map as consistent with an acute-versus-chronic account. It stops short of establishing it: if chronicity were the whole story, transfer should improve on the acute minority of real records, and it does not. Aligning the transported readout's per-record agreement against acuity rank gives $\rho=-0.034$ ($p=0.10$) under the \texttt{source} scaler and $\rho=+0.017$ ($p=0.74$, wrong sign) under \texttt{target}, non-monotone in both, on the 1,490 gradable patients. The acute stratum there is small (61 Stadium~II records), so the test is weak, but it does not support the mechanism, and I do not claim it. \tracenote{CG/floor_audit.json→acuity_strata.acuity_trend (rho -0.0337 p_one_sided 0.0970 / +0.0170 p 0.7431, n_patients 1490); acuity counts Table 3.2 (1617 of 4324, 90.23 pct); claim strength: L in 03_claim_evidence_map.md}

The dataset's own validation saw the symptom of all this without the location. \citet{gillette2023medalcare} validated \medalcare\ by overlaying feature distributions on a real reference and by a clinician Turing test, in which cardiologists diagnosed the pathology of real ECGs at 62\,\% and of simulated ones at 39\,\%. A drop of that size is what one expects if the channels clinicians read for diagnosis carry less information in the simulated signal, which is what the audit measures feature by feature. Their published check of feature distributions was qualitative and class-level, with hand-picked canonical features per pathology; nothing in it could have localised the deficit, and nothing in it did. The audit is the instrument their validation lacked, not a contradiction of it. \tracenote{gillette2023medalcare (62 vs 39 pct, Fig 6 protocol); framing per fence #25}

The audit needs the feature matrix and the labels of both cohorts, and nothing else: no encoder, no training, no transfer experiment. On this data it runs in minutes on a CPU, and every entry comes with a bootstrap interval.

Whether its map means anything for a downstream model is an empirical question. Section~\ref{sec:res-predict} tests the map as a prediction of which channels survive transport; Section~\ref{sec:res-repair} tests whether anything downstream can substitute for the information the map says is absent. \tracenote{instrument character: MR C.4; scripts analysis/fidelity_audit.py (CPU, seeds in header)}

\section[The audit predicts transfer]{The audit predicts which channels transfer}
\label{sec:res-predict}
% v0 DRAFT 22 Aug (D3). Numbers verified against f2_blocks.json + tmp_v_f2_verify.json before writing.
% Paragraph plan: 01_thesis_structure_v3.md §4.5. Claim rows: P5, P3'' in 03_claim_evidence_map.md.

If the audit's map is right, it should say in advance which parts of the ECG survive the journey from simulator to patient. The test is direct: fit a P2 territory readout on each feature block in \medalcare, score it in-domain on shared run-disjoint folds so the blocks are compared on identical rows, transport it to \ptbxl\ without refitting, and ask how much of the above-floor performance survives. Figure~\ref{fig:blocks} and Table~\ref{tab:f2} give the result under both scaler modes. Under \texttt{source}, the raw transport, the simulator's most informative block fails almost exactly as the audit predicts: \STblock\ scores 0.384 in-domain, the best of the five natural blocks, and lands at 0.155 on \ptbxl, where the constant-predictor floor is 0.153 and the patient-bootstrap interval $[0.147, 0.164]$ contains it. Its transfer efficiency is 0.006. The \Qblock\ block, near-bottom in-domain at 0.264, arrives at 0.238 with an efficiency of 0.612, a hundred times higher. \tracenote{FA/f2_blocks.json→per_block, efficiency, floors; ST CI verifier st_floor_ci [0.1468,0.1641]}

The block orderings carry the statistics. On identical resampled rows, \STblock\ loses to \Qblock\ cross-domain by $-0.083$ (interval $[-0.100, -0.067]$, paired swap $p=0.001$) and to \Rblock\ by $-0.075$ ($[-0.094, -0.056]$, $p=0.001$), while in-domain the same comparison runs the other way by $+0.120$. The reversal between fitting domain and deployment domain is the audit's central claim, observed in a transport experiment that never compares \etasq\ across cohorts. \tracenote{FA/f2_blocks.json→paired_cross (ST-Q -0.0829, ST-R -0.0747), paired_in (ST-Q +0.1205)}

The prediction was written down before the transfer numbers existed. The script that runs this experiment records, ahead of its own results, that \STblock\ would transfer worst and \Qblock\ and \Rblock\ best, on the stated ground that the real domain carries territory where the simulator does not. Ranking the five blocks, simulated informativeness anti-ranks \texttt{source} efficiency at $\rho=-0.90$; with only five blocks the exact one-sided permutation $p$ is 0.042, the two-sided value 0.083 misses 0.05, and the correlation is fragile to the cross-validation seed (a re-run under a different fold seed gives $-0.70$, one-sided $p=0.117$), so I treat the paired orderings above, not the $\rho$, as the load-bearing result. Two supporting facts sharpen it. Real informativeness ranks \texttt{source} efficiency perfectly, $\rho=+1.00$ (exact $p=0.017$): cross-domain performance follows what the channels carry in reality, so a readout trained on the simulator loads on exactly the channels reality leaves empty. And simulated informativeness anti-ranks the raw cross-domain scores themselves at $\rho=-0.80$, so the effect is not an artefact of dividing by in-domain performance. \tracenote{FA/f2_blocks.json→pre_stated_prediction; verifier spearman_exact (0.0417/0.0833; seed7 -0.70 p 0.1167; eta2_real vs eff rho 1.0; eta2_sim vs raw F1 -0.8)}

The \texttt{target} mode marks the boundary of the prediction. Diagonal recalibration erases the rank correlation ($\rho=+0.10$) and lifts \STblock\ from the floor to 0.230 (efficiency 0.296), yet \STblock\ remains second-worst of the five blocks after recalibration. Per-column rescaling repairs the part of the failure that is scale mismatch; it cannot supply territory information the channel does not carry in the target domain. The prediction is therefore a claim about raw transport, and both modes are reported wherever a transport number appears. \qone{Which mode is the primary reading of a transport claim is a declared open question (\S\ref{sec:transport-method}).} \tracenote{FA/f2_blocks.json→spearman (target rho +0.10); efficiency ST 0.006→0.296; ST remains 2nd-worst under target (0.230 < Q 0.241 < globals/T/full54)}

Fitting on the simulator can destroy a channel that works without fitting. The two R-amplitude columns that define the frontal axis, given to the same ridge readout and fitted on \medalcare, transport at 0.175--0.178 under the two modes; they reach only 0.175 in-domain, barely above the 0.126 floor, because the simulator has almost no axis information to fit (\S\ref{sec:res-audit}). The same two measurements used with no fitting at all, by assigning each real record's raw axis angle to the nearest territory anchor, score 0.304, and a readout fitted on \ptbxl\ itself reaches 0.300. The information is in the channel and the anchor frame; what the simulator adds is a coefficient vector pointing at the wrong thing. \tracenote{FA/f2_blocks.json→per_block.axis2 (0.1751/0.1747/0.1778); f3_repair.json→axis_reconciliation naive 0.30431; real-fitted CV 0.2997 MR C.2}

The audit also makes a positive prediction, and it holds. The T block is the one block the audit scores as informativeness-matched (\etasq\ 0.032 simulated, 0.028 real), and after recalibration it is the best-transferring single block, at 0.301 within 0.012 of the full 54-feature set (paired $p=0.22$). Where the simulator writes territory information into a channel at the strength reality does, that channel transfers. \tracenote{FA/f2_blocks.json→per_block.T_amp cross_target 0.3012; paired_cross cross_target:full54_vs_T_amp +0.0115 [-0.0069,+0.0293] p 0.221}

The map, then, predicts raw transport at block level, in both directions. What it cannot say is whether the deficit it measures can be worked around downstream, with the latent in hand: that is the next section's question. \tracenote{bridge}

\section[The limits of repair]{What transfers, what does not, and why: the limits of repair}
\label{sec:res-repair}
% v0 DRAFT 22 Aug (D3). Numbers verified against f3_repair.json + tmp_v_f3-channel-repair_out.txt before writing.
% Paragraph plan: 01_thesis_structure_v3.md §4.6. Claim rows: P6, P3 in 03_claim_evidence_map.md.

If the simulator's territory signal lives in the wrong channels, an obvious repair suggests itself: restrict the latent readout to the channels the audit certifies as faithful. Figure~\ref{fig:repair} and Table~\ref{tab:f3} show what happens. A ridge map from the latent to the 54 features, fitted on \medalcare\ only, defines for each block a low-dimensional representation through which the readout is forced; \ptbxl\ features are never used. Restricted to the Q and R channels (24 dimensions), the readout scores 0.285 cross-domain against 0.340 for the unrestricted 1024-dimensional readout, a paired difference of $-0.055$ (interval $[-0.075, -0.037]$, $p<0.001$) under the \texttt{target} mode. The single-block restrictions do worse still (Q $-0.027$, $p=0.01$; R $-0.113$; ST $-0.141$, both $p<0.001$), and the ST restriction sits at or below the bulk of its own random-projection null ($p=0.88$ in the direction of beating it). The audit's ordering survives inside the failure: Q+R beats ST by $+0.086$ ($p<0.001$; $+0.078$ under \texttt{source}). Knowing which channels are faithful does not buy transfer, because restriction can only discard information, and what the faithful channels carry is already inside the 1024 dimensions. One restriction reaches parity, and it is worth describing precisely because of what it is. The twelve latent dimensions predicting the four measures in the three inferior leads (II, III, aVF) score 0.343 against the unrestricted 0.340 ($+0.003$, interval $[-0.018, +0.024]$, $p=0.79$; under \texttt{source} $-0.003$, $p=0.76$). This cell passes the norm-matched random-projection null ($p=0.005$; an independent re-implementation gives $p=0.0099$, and both survive a Bonferroni correction over the eight arms tested) and the shuffled-label refit null ($p=0.005$, though that null is degenerate here and certifies only that the readout uses the simulated angle). More tellingly, it beats a readout on the \emph{measured} inferior-lead features by $+0.044$ ($[+0.022, +0.066]$, $p<0.001$; the measured features alone score 0.300), so the latent's twelve inferior-lead dimensions carry something the extracted features do not. It is the best of eight arms and is reported as parity under that selection, not as an improvement: everything the 1024-dimensional latent transfers about territory fits in twelve dimensions aimed at the inferior leads. \tracenote{FA/f3_repair.json→blocks.inferior 0.3427, paired.inferior_vs_unrestricted_{target,source}, nulls.inferior_randproj p 0.00498 (verifier 0.0099), inferior_shufphi p 0.005 degenerate (null mass at 0.1366); verifier competitor 0.2995 delta +0.0441 [+0.0219,+0.0658]}

The falsification arm asks whether the deficit is covariate shift after all: if the simulator carries the right information at the wrong input distribution, reweighting its training rows toward the real feature distribution should help. It hurts. Weighting \medalcare\ rows by a domain classifier on the two axis-defining features and refitting, transfer drops by $-0.073$ ($[-0.086, -0.061]$, $p<0.001$) against the unweighted readout on the same rows; the effective sample size falls to 1,120 of 6,513, and a control that resamples uniformly to the same effective size scores 0.329 against the weighted 0.273, so the harm is the weighting itself, not the lost rows. A six-feature weighting summary harms by a similar amount ($-0.069$, $p<0.001$), but its effective sample size collapses to 208 of 4,968 and most of that harm is attributable to the collapse, so I rest the conclusion on the axis-pair arm. Emphasising the rows of the simulator that look most like reality moves the readout away from what reality needs, which is what one expects when the missing ingredient is information rather than distribution. \tracenote{FA/f3_repair.json→reweight.axpair (-0.0732 [-0.0859,-0.0609], ESS 1120/6513), reweight.six (-0.0690, ESS 208/4968); verifier ESS-matched control 0.3287 vs weighted 0.2734; caveat per forbidden #21}

The sensitivity encoder reproduces the pattern: on \texttt{exp8\_leadfix\_dual} the Q+R restriction loses by $-0.064$ and the inferior-lead cell sits at $+0.0004$ ($p=0.96$). \tracenote{FA/f3_repair.json→exp8_leadfix_dual.paired}

Taken together, the three experiments say the deficit is missing information, not misplaced information. The audit measures an absence in the synthetic signal; transport tracks what the channels carry in reality and not what the simulator believes about them; and no downstream operation, not channel selection, not recalibration, not reweighting, substitutes for what was never written into the waveform. What remains after every repair is a representation-level fact: the two-dimensional planes from which territory is read out in the two domains are close to orthogonal in the latent space (normalised principal-angle overlap 0.008, interval $[0.002, 0.017]$, against split-half ceilings near 0.31--0.36), even though local neighbourhood structure corresponds in part (a nearest-neighbour transfer reaches 32--42\,\% of its ceiling). The latent is not unrelated across domains; the linear directions that encode territory are. \tracenote{2026-08-12 tier1 §2.4 (normalised overlap 0.0076 [0.0016,0.0168], ceilings 0.360/0.311) and §2.5 (kNN 32-42 pct of ceiling), both survive the 08-13 audit (MR C.4); dossier C Card I}

What does transfer is small, coherent and identifiable: a twelve-dimensional inferior-lead subspace at parity with the full latent, and a zero-shot anchor frame that the axis reaches without any fitting. Both point the same way. The transferable content is the part of the simulator's territory encoding that overlaps with where reality writes territory, and enlarging it is not an encoder problem. A simulator that wrote territory into the Q and R waves and the frontal axis at the strength the real cohort shows would give a readout something to carry; measuring exactly which channels need that correction is what the audit is for. \tracenote{P3/P6 composition; MR C.4}
