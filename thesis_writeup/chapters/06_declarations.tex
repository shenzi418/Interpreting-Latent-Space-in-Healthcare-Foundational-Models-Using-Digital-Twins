\section{Use of Generative AI}
\label{sec:decl-ai}

In this work I used the generative AI assistant \emph{Claude} (model family Claude Opus; publisher Anthropic, \url{https://www.anthropic.com}; accessed through the Claude Code agentic coding tool, \url{https://claude.com/claude-code}, and \url{https://claude.ai}) throughout the project, from methodology design to the drafting of this thesis. Two auxiliary tools were called from within that environment: PapersFlow (bibliographic look-up and citation verification) and the Brave Search API (web search). This section discloses the use against the list of research subtasks in the ICLR~2027 author guidelines, as required by the MRes regulations.

\paragraph{Tasks with required disclosure.}
I used generative AI tools to \emph{design and provide feedback on research methodology and experiments} (experimental protocols, pre-registered decision rules, the construction of null distributions and control arms, and the design of the informativeness-fidelity audit); to \emph{implement methods} (the large majority of the training, feature-extraction and analysis code in the accompanying repository was written with the assistant, then run, inspected and debugged by me); and to \emph{clean and reformat datasets} (dataset manifests, split generation, the lead-order and normalisation fixes, and the hand-crafted feature tables). I used them to \emph{propose and refine hypotheses} (for example, that the simulator and the clinical cohort encode infarct territory in different ECG measure-blocks, and that a per-feature audit would predict transfer); to \emph{help develop the conceptual framework} of the thesis (the distinction between marginal realism and informativeness fidelity, and the audit\,$\rightarrow$\,prediction\,$\rightarrow$\,repair structure). I used them to \emph{formulate mathematical claims and provide ingredients for their justification} (the constant-predictor floor of the circular mean resultant length, its characterisation as the supremum over label-independent predictors, and the identity placing permutation nulls below that floor; the statements were checked numerically by me and their derivations are elementary). And I used them to \emph{interpret results}: results were interpreted in an iterative dialogue with the assistant, and the final interpretation, and every claim in this thesis, is mine. I did \emph{not} use generative AI tools to generate synthetic datasets: every synthetic ECG analysed here comes from the published MedalCare-XL dataset produced by its authors with a biophysical simulator, and no AI-generated data of any kind was used. Assistance with translation, and support for qualitative or thematic data analysis, are not applicable to this work.

\paragraph{Tasks with recommended disclosure.}
I also used generative AI tools to create and edit software code; to suggest experimental parameters (regularisation grids, bootstrap and permutation sizes, cross-validation layouts); to create and modify scientific figures and tables from the saved result files; to draft parts of this thesis and to edit it for readability; to summarise and analyse existing literature, identify relevant literature and research gaps, brainstorm, source information, and format references; and to suggest the structure of the thesis and candidate section titles.

\paragraph{Review of AI-assisted work.}
I have reviewed all AI-assisted work, in five specific ways. Every quantitative result reported here was produced by scripts that I ran, archived in the public repository at the tagged commit named in Section~\ref{sec:decl-data}. The central results (the fidelity audit, the transfer prediction and the repair experiments) were re-implemented from scratch in an independent verification pass; where that pass corrected a number, the corrected value is the one reported. In a final integrity pass, every number in the text was traced back to the on-disk result file that produced it, and a fixed list of over-claims identified during the project was searched for and removed from every draft. Every reference in the bibliography was verified against a bibliographic database before it was cited; no citation was reproduced from the assistant's memory. And all AI-drafted prose was read, edited and approved by me. I take responsibility for the final content of this work, including all text, claims and artefacts produced with the aid of generative AI.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:decl-ethics}

This project did not require ethics approval and none was sought: it uses no data collected by me, involves no human participants, and processes only (a)~PTB-XL, a publicly released and de-identified clinical ECG dataset distributed through PhysioNet under its own institutional ethics arrangements, and (b)~MedalCare-XL, a fully synthetic dataset generated by electrophysiological simulation, which contains no data from any real person. No attempt at re-identification was made and no record-level clinical data leave the analysis environment. No formal ethics review was recorded earlier in the project because the data situation was clear from the outset; I state this here for completeness. \todo{owner: confirm this wording matches what was (not) filed with the supervisor}

The ethical risks I considered concern the use of the \emph{results} rather than of the data. First, a negative sim-to-real result can be over-read as ``digital twins are not useful''; the thesis argues the opposite (that a synthetic cohort should be audited for the information a downstream task needs before it is used, and that this audit is cheap), and the text is written to resist that misreading. Second, the audit itself could be misused to select only the channels on which a simulator looks faithful and thereby over-sell it; the thesis therefore reports the full per-feature map, including the channels on which the simulator is uninformative, and reports the failed repair arms alongside the one that reached parity. Third, any model or readout described here is a research artefact and is not intended for, or validated for, clinical use. The broader impact I hope for is a safer validation practice for synthetic-data pipelines in cardiology.

\section{Sustainability}
\label{sec:decl-sustain}

The computational footprint of this project was kept small by design. I reused a publicly available pre-trained foundation model (ECGFounder) rather than pre-training one; all fine-tuning runs adapted a frozen backbone with lightweight residual adapters and a linear (or small bottleneck) head for at most 30 epochs each; and the entire analysis stage (feature extraction, the fidelity audit, all transfer and repair experiments, and the circular-statistics evaluations) runs on CPU from cached latent representations, so no GPU time was spent on evaluation. No large hyper-parameter sweeps were run, and an explicit experimental freeze at the end of the research phase prevented redundant re-runs. All training was performed on a single consumer GPU (NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5080, 16\,GB) in a personal workstation. No run logged its wall-clock time, so I bounded each run by the modification-time span of its output directory. The twenty fine-tuning runs that survive on disk (March--August 2026) account for roughly 9~GPU-hours in total; latent export and the lead-permutation inference sweep add a further 3--4. Allowing the same order of magnitude again for early exploratory runs whose outputs were later deleted, I estimate the project's total GPU usage at roughly 25~GPU-hours, with 40 as an upper estimate. That is of the order of 10\,kWh of electricity and 1--2\,kg~CO$_2$e at typical UK grid intensity, an order-of-magnitude statement rather than a measurement. \tracenote{thesis_writeup/notes/gpu_hours_estimate.md}
